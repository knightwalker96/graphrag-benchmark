01 — The brief

Prove the graph earns its tokens — on data you didn't pick

LLMs burn thousands of tokens to answer questions over large document sets. The benchmark's bet is that a knowledge graph makes that cheaper and better than vector search alone. Three pipelines answer the same 50 questions over the same corpus, and the measurements decide.

Pipeline 1

LLM-only

Question in, answer out. No retrieval at all — the floor everything else must clear.

Pipeline 2

Basic RAG

Vector embeddings retrieve the most similar chunks. Today's industry standard — and the real baseline to beat.

Pipeline 3

GraphRAG

A TigerGraph knowledge graph resolves entities and relationships; the LLM only writes the final answer.

400 filings nobody has memorized

Annual reports, event filings and proxy statements for 100 S&P-100 companies — deliberately synthetic and dated into FY2025–26, so no language model can answer from prior knowledge. Retrieval has to actually work.

07 — What transfers

Four things worth carrying into your own pipeline

01

Know which questions a graph is for

Graphs win when the answer is a set scattered across many documents. They add little on single-fact lookups — plain vector search beat our graph 8/8 to 5/8 there. Audit your real question mix before building one, and route per question rather than forcing every query down the same path.

02

Cheaper and better aren't opposites

The usual assumption is that cutting context costs accuracy. Sending structure instead of raw text — a matched list plus one evidence sentence each — cut tokens by half and raised accuracy at the same time. The saving comes from precision, not from truncation.

03

Your graph is only as good as its extraction

One pass over everything gets you coverage but noise. Adding a second, narrow pass that re-reads the right section of the right document for the handful of facts queries actually depend on took auditor coverage from 28 to 100 companies. Recall first, then precision where it counts.

04

Make the comparison one you'd trust from a rival

Tune the baseline as hard as your own system, hold out a test set you never look at, repeat every run, and make failures loud rather than silently falling back. A benchmark that can't embarrass you isn't measuring anything.
